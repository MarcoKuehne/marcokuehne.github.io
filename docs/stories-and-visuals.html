Chapter 2 Stories and Visuals

Stories make data and numbers memorable. Stories are everywhere. When you think about the context of the data, stories evolve naturally: how does this data connect to you, your friends and family, your work, or society as a whole?

Most of this chapter follows a single data set: dog breeds, each scored on intelligence, longevity, health, cost, appetite and grooming, and each ranked by how popular it is in the United States.7 The same numbers will be shown as a sortable table, as a plain chart, as an award-winning poster, and finally taken apart to see what a graphic is really made of. Near the end we switch to a second, smaller data set — the number of students in seven terms of this course — because stripping a chart down works better on something with seven numbers than on something with eighty-seven.

A word about the file before we use it. It lists 174 breeds, but not every breed has every measurement: popularity comes from American Kennel Club registrations, lifespan from survey data, prices from a puppy marketplace, and these sources do not cover the same animals. 87 breeds carry both a data score and a popularity rank, and those 87 are what the chapter works with. Missing data is a subject in its own right, and it gets its own chapter later; for now it is enough to notice that "the data set" already meant a decision somebody made.

2.1 Numbers, words and pictures

A good story begins with facts, and facts usually arrive as a table. Here is the whole thing — all 87 breeds, nothing hidden, nothing summarised. Click a column heading to sort it.

Now use it. Which breed has the highest data score? Sort the score column: Border Collie, 3.64. Which is the most popular? Sort the other one: Labrador Retriever, rank 1. Both answers are exact, both take two seconds, and no chart on earth would have given them to you faster.

This is worth saying plainly, because books like this one usually rush past it: for looking a value up, and for ranking on one column, a table beats every chart. If that is the question, do not draw anything.

Nor should everything become a picture. The two sentences you just read carry four numbers between them, and a chart of four numbers wastes the reader's time — it takes a paragraph of space to deliver what a clause delivers. Prose can also do something no chart can: it can state the point. "The Border Collie scores highest and is only the 45th most popular breed" is a finding. A scatterplot showing the same thing is evidence for a finding, and leaves the reader to work out which one. Good analysis usually needs all three registers — the number for precision, the sentence for meaning, the picture for shape — and the skill is knowing which job each one is doing.

2.1.1 Where all three stop

Here are two questions about the same file.

Do Americans own the good dogs? Is a better-scoring breed more likely to be popular?

Do big dogs die younger? Is a taller breed shorter-lived?

Neither is about one column. Each asks whether two columns move together, and a table shows one order at a time. Still, sorting is the right instinct, so let us try it — sort by the first column of each pair and read down the second.

Two shrugs. Both columns wobble. Neither settles anything — and note that you are looking at ten rows out of 87 and 76, which is all a printed table ever gives you: a window, never the shape.

Now draw them.

One convention first, because it decides how the left-hand picture reads. Popularity is a rank: 1 is the most registered breed in America, 160 the least, so a small number means a popular dog. The axis is therefore drawn upside down, rank 1 at the top, which makes "up" mean "more popular" — the direction a reader expects without being told.

Figure 2.1: The same two pairs of columns, drawn. One second each.

One second each, and they are not remotely the same picture. The left panel is a cloud: quality tells you nothing about popularity. The right panel falls away steeply: the Irish Wolfhound stands 81 cm at the shoulder and lives under seven years, the Chihuahua stands 13 cm and lives sixteen and a half.

That is the argument. The two sorted tables felt equally inconclusive. The two charts are not alike at all. A table cannot distinguish "there is nothing here" from "there is something enormous here", because both look like a column of wobbling numbers — and if you cannot tell those two apart, you cannot use the table to decide anything.

Nothing was added to the data between the tables and the charts. Every number in the pictures was already in the file. What changed is that the comparison moved from your patience into your eyes.

Numbers, words and pictures are not three different things. A picture is a fact, arranged so that a human can perceive it; a sentence is a fact, arranged so that a human can remember it.

Which means a badly arranged visual is not merely ugly. It is a fact the reader cannot reach.

2.2 Telling a story

Back to the dogs, and to the question that got nowhere. A cloud with no pattern sounds like a dead end. It is not — it is the story.

Figure 2.2: All 87 breeds at once. The grey lines mark the two averages, not zero.

The answer arrives in about one second, and it is no. The cloud does not run from bottom-left to top-right. If popularity tracked quality, it would.

And now the story, which is in the corners.

The Border Collie has the highest data score of any breed, 3.64, and sits only at rank 45 in popularity. The Border Terrier scores 3.61 and ranks 80th — barely half as owned as a dog it beats on every measure. Meanwhile the Bulldog is the sixth most popular dog in America with a score of 0.99, the lowest of any breed people buy in numbers at all.

That is a story, and it did not come from a storyteller. It came from two columns and a scatterplot. Somebody who wanted to could now ask why: bulldogs are expensive, short-lived and prone to serious illness, and none of that stops people wanting one. Quality is not what makes a dog popular. Something else is — and naming that something is where the analysis would go next.

2.2.1 Reading the axes

Two things in that picture need explaining before you trust it, and neither is obvious.

The vertical axis is a rank, not a quantity. It counts positions, not dogs. Rank 1 is the Labrador Retriever, rank 160 is somebody's beloved and very rare hound. The distance from rank 1 to rank 2 is not the same number of actual dogs as the distance from rank 100 to rank 101 — not remotely. A rank throws away the size of the gaps and keeps only the order. That is a real loss, and it is also why ranks are so common: they survive being merged from sources that measured different things.

Because a low number means more popular, the axis is drawn upside down, so that "up" means what a reader expects it to mean. Look at the tick labels: 1st at the top, 150th at the bottom.

There is no origin. A quantity has a meaningful zero — zero euros, zero students, zero grams. A rank does not. No dog has rank 0, and no dog has a data score of 0 either, because the score is built from percentile components that never bottom out. So the crossing lines cannot sit at (0, 0). They sit at the two averages: a data score of 2.6 and a popularity rank of 59.3.

That choice has a consequence you should hold on to: the four regions are defined relative to this set of 87 breeds. Add fifty more breeds and the lines move.

The 2 × 2 matrix

Cut two variables at one point each, and you get four boxes. Give the boxes names, and you have turned a cloud of points into an argument.

It is one of the most reused devices in all of analysis. The BCG growth–share matrix splits market growth against market share and calls the corners stars, cash cows, question marks, dogs — genuinely, dogs. The Eisenhower matrix splits urgent against important. A confusion matrix splits what a model predicted against what was true, and its four boxes are the true positives, true negatives, false positives and false negatives on which every accuracy measure in this book is built. A 2 × 2 contingency table does the same for two yes/no variables.

The device is cheap and it is powerful, and it has one honest cost: the cut is a decision. A breed one hundredth of a point above the line gets a flattering name; the breed just below it does not. The names are a summary of the picture, never a substitute for it.

Table 2.2: How the 87 breeds divide between the four corners.
Quadrant Breeds
Popular and good 27
Popular but poor 21
Good but overlooked 20
Neither 19

Count them, because the counts make the same point the picture did. The four corners are almost equally full: 27, 21, 20, 19. That near-tie is the finding. If quality drove popularity, two corners would be crowded and two nearly empty; instead a dog is about as likely to be good-and-ignored as good-and-loved. Table 2.2 is the scatterplot with the shape thrown away — which is exactly why you needed the scatterplot.

2.2.2 The designed version

The same data — the same file, in fact — was turned into a poster by David McCandless and Information is Beautiful.8

Figure 2.3: Best in Show: The Ultimate Data Dog, by Information is Beautiful. The same 87 breeds as the scatterplot above.

This is the takeaway of the section, so slow down and take the poster apart. Underneath the illustration it is the same two-dimensional scatterplot you just read — and everything beyond those two dimensions has been smuggled in through some other property of the mark.

Variable Visual channel In ggplot2
Data score horizontal position aes(x = )
Popularity rank vertical position aes(y = )
Kennel-club group colour of the silhouette aes(colour = )
Size category size of the silhouette aes(size = )
Intelligence the direction the dog faces —
Breed the silhouette itself, plus its printed name aes(shape = ), geom_text()

The first four are ordinary. Colour, size and shape are exactly the aesthetics you will map variables onto in any plotting grammar, and the top-right of the poster carries a key for each one, the way a legend should.

The fifth is not ordinary at all. The dog faces left if it is dumb and right if it is clever — the key spells it out, with two little silhouettes labelled dumb and clever. No plotting library offers "direction faced" as an aesthetic. It exists here only because somebody drew every dog by hand, and it is free: the silhouette had to point somewhere.

Look at what that means. Intelligence is on this poster twice — once folded into the horizontal position, and once again in which way the animal is looking. The designer decided it mattered enough to say twice.

And then a channel that is not a mark at all: which named corner a dog falls into — Inexplicably Overrated, Hot Dogs!, The Rightly Ignored, Overlooked Treasures. That naming does real work. It converts a statistical pattern into a claim somebody can argue with, which is exactly what a bare scatterplot never manages.

A flat sheet of paper has exactly two dimensions, and position is the strongest channel you have — which is why the two variables you most want compared should go on the axes and nowhere else.

Everything after that is a compromise: colour, size, shape, orientation, labels, named regions. Each one works, each one is weaker than position, and each one needs a key or it carries nothing.

2.2.3 What the horizontal axis is hiding

There is one more legend, headed our data score, and it is the important one. Six icons: intelligence, costs, longevity, grooming, ailments, appetite. Six variables, squeezed into the horizontal axis before the drawing ever began.

Composite score

A composite score (or index) combines several variables into a single number, so that one column can stand in for many.

You already live with dozens of them: a school grade average, the Human Development Index, a credit score, body mass index, a customer satisfaction score. Each one buys you the same thing — a single axis you can sort, plot and argue about — and each one costs you the same thing: the components disappear, and so do the weights somebody chose for them.

The poster shows you the result and not the recipe. The file has the recipe:

\[\text{score} = \text{intelligence} + \text{longevity} + \text{ailments} + \tfrac{1}{2}\left(\text{costs} + \text{appetite} + \text{grooming}\right)\]

Every component is already a percentile between 0 and 1, so each can contribute at most 1 point, or at most ½ a point for the three halved ones. Two of the poster's words are gentler than the spreadsheet's: costs is the average purchase price of a puppy, and appetite is the annual food bill.

Do it by hand for three dogs — the best, a famous one, and the worst.

Table 2.3: Three dogs, six percentiles each, and the score they add up to.
Breed intel. longev. ailm. costs appet. groom. Published
Border Collie 1.00 0.79 0.78 0.71 0.68 0.75 3.64
Beagle 0.09 0.72 0.89 1.00 0.68 0.50 2.79
Bulldog 0.03 0.01 0.44 0.01 0.23 0.75 0.99

Add the first three columns, then add half of the last three.

  • Border Collie: 1.00 + 0.79 + 0.78 = 2.57, and half of (0.71 + 0.68 + 0.75 = 2.14) is 1.07. Total 3.64.
  • Beagle: 0.09 + 0.72 + 0.89 = 1.70, and half of 2.18 is 1.09. Total 2.79.
  • Bulldog: 0.03 + 0.01 + 0.44 = 0.48, and half of 0.99 is 0.50. Total 0.98.

Three sums you can do in your head, and all three land on the published figure. Across all 87 breeds the largest disagreement is 0.015 — the rounding of the printed percentages, nothing more. The highest score anyone could reach is 1 + 1 + 1 + ½ × 3 = 4.5, exactly the maximum the file states.

Now look at what the arithmetic just made visible.

The weights are a choice. Intelligence, longevity and health count twice what price, food and grooming count. Nobody voted on that. Weight all six equally and dogs change corners.

And the Beagle is the whole argument in one row. It is the least intelligent breed in the file — 9th percentile — and it still scores 2.79, above average, because it lives long, is healthy, and is cheap to buy. A composite score lets a variable you care about be outvoted by five you were not thinking about.

A reader of the poster cannot ask this. A reader of the spreadsheet can.

The best data dog is not a dog

Somewhere on that poster, among 87 carefully drawn breeds with their percentiles and their kennel-club colours, there is a cat.

It is not in the data. It has no score, no popularity rank and no group. It is there because somebody who spent weeks reconciling puppy prices from puppyfind.com decided the reader deserved a joke.

Keep that in mind for your own work. Nothing in this chapter says a graphic has to be humourless — only that the reader must be able to tell which parts are the data. The cat is safe precisely because nobody could mistake it for a finding.

Design that makes a reader look longer is worth its ink. Design that makes a reader unable to check is not.

Story before design. Design only where it helps understanding.

And somewhere in the poster there is a cat.

2.3 Less is more

Figure 2.3 is a masterpiece of art and data science, and it is also the exception. It earns its complexity because it is a poster: something you stand in front of, in a museum or on a wall, and give five minutes to. Almost nothing you will make is a poster. A chart in a report, a slide, a paper or a dashboard gets a few seconds and must answer one question in them — and for that, everything the poster spends is a cost. From here on we go the other way: not how much can a graphic carry, but how little can it get away with.

Edward Tufte gave that principle a name.9

Definition

The data-ink ratio is the proportion of the ink in a graphic that presents actual data, relative to the total ink used.

Tufte's prescription follows: show the data, maximise the data-ink ratio, erase non-data ink.

Most charts fail this test not because anybody decided to clutter them, but because the software defaults did it for them. Below is one chart — the number of participants in seven terms of this course — taken apart one element at a time.

Drag the slider, or click it and use the arrow keys. Twenty-two steps. Nothing is ever added except at step 17.

Nothing was added between step 1 and step 22 except the numbers at step 17. Everything else was taken away: a frame, two background fills, two sets of gridlines, bar outlines, seven colours that encoded nothing, a legend that repeated the axis labels, an axis title, bold type, tick marks, and finally the y-axis itself.

The bars stay in the order of the terms rather than being sorted from tallest to shortest. Why?

2.3.1 What the ink is worth

Data-ink can be measured. Save each version as an image, count the pixels that are not white, and you have the ink the graphic actually spends.

Step Ink (pixels) Reduction
1 everything the defaults offer 307,390 —
4 backgrounds removed 98,264 68 %
11 colour and legend removed 99,825 68 %
22 data and labels only 93,383 70 %

The final chart spends 70 % less ink and carries exactly the same seven numbers. Two-thirds of the original was decoration, most of it gone in the first four steps. Note that step 11 uses slightly more ink than step 4 while looking cleaner — dropping the legend gave the panel more room, so the bars grew. Ink is a proxy, not a goal.

Tufte was writing in 1983, about paper, when the metaphor was nearly literal. So take it literally.

A six-by-nine-inch page is about 0.035 m². Offset printing lays down an ink film of roughly one micrometre — around 1.2 grams per square metre at full coverage — and black offset ink costs on the order of ten to fifteen euros per kilogram. Replacing one bar chart with a leaner version saves about 0.0013 grams per copy.

For a 500-copy academic monograph that is roughly one cent, for every chart in the book. For a 50,000-copy bestseller, under one euro.

These are order-of-magnitude estimates, and the direction is the point: the data-ink ratio cannot be defended on economic grounds. On paper it is worth cents. On a screen ink is free and the saving is exactly zero.

So the principle must rest on something else, and it does. Non-data ink costs no money; it costs the reader's attention, which is the genuinely scarce resource. Every element competes for someone who will give the graphic a few seconds.

2.4 Never use pie charts

That is about what a graphic spends. There is a second question: what the human eye can actually do.

The American Kennel Club sorts every breed into one of seven groups. Our 87 dogs divide up like this.

Two questions. Which group has the most breeds? And are there more hounds or more toy breeds?

Take a moment before you scroll.

Can you tell now?

Sporting, with 15. And there are exactly as many hounds as toy breeds — 13 each. Both charts show the same seven numbers. The second answers both questions instantly; the first cannot answer either, and the tie is invisible in it. The reason is perceptual, not aesthetic: a pie asks you to compare angles and areas, a bar asks you to compare positions along a common scale.

Notice also what makes this example hard. The seven groups run from 10 to 15 breeds — shares of 11.5 % to 17.2 %. Nothing dominates. That is the ordinary case, and it is precisely the case a pie chart cannot serve.

Cleveland and McGill measured this. Position judgements were about twice as accurate as angle judgements, and 88 % of the large errors occurred on the angle task.10

R's own documentation is blunter. The help page for pie() states: "Pie charts are a very bad way of displaying information."

When a pie chart is defensible

Two or three slices, one obviously dominant, and the point being part of a whole rather than a comparison between parts.

Recent work is gentler than the folklore: pies, donuts and bars performed equally well for extracting part–whole relationships, and the differences that appeared were often small. Bars won on ranking and were faster overall.11

The rule is not "pie charts are evil." It is: the moment a reader has to rank the slices, use bars.

2.5 Then why use bars at all?

Follow the argument to its end and it turns on the bar chart itself. A bar spends a lot of ink to place one number: a filled rectangle from zero to the value, when only the tip carries information. If position along a scale is what the eye reads well, a dot at the same height would do the same job.

Figure 2.4: The ten best-scoring breeds, twice. The dots carry the same information.

That is Cleveland's dot plot.12 Ten breeds, ten numbers, a fraction of the ink — and nothing lost.

Meanwhile a survey of 703 physiology papers found 85.6 % contained at least one bar graph.13

So: technically we could plot almost anything. Empirically, people plot bars. What lies between? Three things are worth keeping before you delete every bar you own.

Bars carry the zero. A dot plot frees you from the baseline — which is exactly the risk. Truncating an axis biases judgement in any encoding, even when the truncation is visibly marked.14

Figure 2.5: Same five breeds, same scores, two axes.

Bars mean "compare these separate things." Readers reliably associate bars with discrete comparisons and lines with trends, without ever being taught to.15 That association is not decoration — it is a claim about the data.

Which gives a rule you may already know, and probably follow without thinking: never join the dots across categories that have no order. Easy to state. The figure below is why it holds, and it is worth seeing once, because the rule is usually taught as etiquette when it is really about truth.

Here are the seven kennel-club groups, drawn three ways.

Figure 2.6: The same seven numbers. Only the middle and right panels disagree with each other.

The middle panel invents a story: a dip at non-sporting, a peak at sporting, a recovery. The right-hand panel tells a completely different story — a steady climb — from the same seven numbers. Only the order of the categories changed, and that order is arbitrary, because "toy" does not come before or after "hound" in any sense the world cares about. The bars are identical under reordering; the line is a different claim every time.

A line asserts that the space between two points means something. Between "terrier" and "toy" there is nothing. That is the whole content of the rule: you may join the dots exactly when the order along the axis is real, because only then does "between" exist.

Which also says when you may. The seven terms of the course did happen one after another — SS 2020 really does come before WS 2020/21 — so joining those dots is legitimate, and a line there says something the bars cannot: it went up, then down, then up again. Reorder those seven and you have not made a different picture, you have made a wrong one. We come back to this at the end of the section.

Familiarity is real, and small. The conventional vertical bar is identified faster than horizontal bars or lines — by roughly 35 to 55 milliseconds.16

Figure 2.7: Breeds per kennel-club group. The vertical version is recognised faster; the horizontal version reads its labels more easily.

That is an advantage in recognition speed, not in accuracy of reading values — and the horizontal version buys something back, since its labels do not have to be tilted.

Technically possible: almost any encoding, animated, interactive, three-dimensional.

Actually used: bars and pies, because that is what spreadsheets offer. Across 42,000 web charts, the bar share ranged from 12 % to 57 % depending only on which tool made them.17

The defensible middle: the most conventional form that supports the judgement your reader must make. Bars for separate categories with a meaningful zero. Dots when zero is not meaningful, labels are long, or many series compete. Lines for trends.

And spend what you saved on the thing that helps most: put the message in the title and label the data directly, so nobody has to translate a legend before they can think.
